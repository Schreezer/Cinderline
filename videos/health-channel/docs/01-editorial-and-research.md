# Editorial and research

## The brief

Make useful, science-based health explainers for everyday adults. A short resolves one specific question and leaves a practical takeaway. The film uses off-screen narration, original illustrations, generated infographics, and HTML animation. Humor can come from a situation or a prop; it is optional, not a requirement to turn every topic into a joke.

The user explicitly disliked the detox episode's writing. The next script should be judged on its own spoken quality. A rendered video, complete transcript, or passing check does not establish that the script is good.

## Writing sequence

1. Name the viewer's question and the one change in understanding the film should deliver.
2. Check the queue and existing episodes for duplicate topics. For a revision, identify the version being improved.
3. Research the claim. Prefer relevant official guidance, systematic reviews, and primary research. Read the actual supporting passages and preserve important limits.
4. Write three different opening angles, then develop the strongest into a complete spoken script. For a 30-second draft, begin around 55–65 words to leave space for pauses; actual narration decides whether it fits.
5. Build a small progression: a recognizable situation, a reveal that changes its meaning, and a useful resolution. Let pictures explain what they can. Avoid repeating an opening joke as the entire ending.
6. Read aloud. Replace stiff explanatory prose, unnecessary setup, and phrases that sound like an advertisement. Use contractions and varied sentence lengths. Leave room after a question or reveal.
7. Supply the script, word count, approximate visual beats, restrained performance notes, and sources. Keep directions and citations outside the spoken text.

For script-only requests, stop at the writing deliverable. When the user authorizes production, proceed within that scope without inventing another approval step.

## Scientific evidence

In each episode's `SOURCES.md`, map every substantive health claim to a URL, source title, relevant finding or section, and applicable qualification. Include retrieval date. Clearly separate established guidance from uncertainty or inference. Generated image text, diagrams, numbers, and captions need the same factual review as narration.

Use fictional product packaging and clearly label illustrative numbers. Avoid implying a single nutrient or ingredient proves a whole food is healthy or unhealthy. General education must not become individualized treatment advice. For the detox topic, distinguish commercial wellness marketing from medical detoxification, and ordinary tea drinking from unsupported cleansing promises.

If browsing is unavailable, identify unverified claims and hold factual readiness. Do not manufacture studies, quotations, or citations. Existing episode sources are starting points, not automatic support for new wording.

## Visual writing

Read the [style study](../references/PhrcWPJEpqs/STYLE-STUDY.md) before storyboarding. Give each beat a visible action or meaningful change: a package turns, evidence appears, a comparison becomes equal, a character changes their mind. Maintain recognizable props and characters. Use diagrams when they explain a relationship more clearly than a character scene.

English and Hindi should be written naturally for their audiences, not translated word for word into an unchanged timeline. Hindi production needs a pronunciation and voice audition before claiming quality. Long-form can repeat question → explanation → exception → practical application across chapters; do not squeeze those chapters into one short.
