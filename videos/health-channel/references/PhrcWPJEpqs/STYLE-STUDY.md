# Illustrated stories for FitSnap

Reference: [The ENTIRE History of Napoleon (NO AI)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PhrcWPJEpqs), History with Dave. Retrieved 23 September 2026. Duration 50:58.

Downloaded at 1280×720. Retrieved English auto-captions, sampled 72 timestamped frames across the timeline, and inspected an additional sequential opening sheet at five-second intervals. Findings below come from those visual samples and transcript passages, not a continuous audiovisual review. Auto-captions contain errors in proper names; they are useful for structure and timing, not historical fact checking. This reference informs presentation, not health claims.

## What makes the presentation work

| Reference interval | Observed technique | FitSnap adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 00:09–00:27 | A geographic map establishes context, a flag scene expresses identity, and a baby illustration introduces the subject. Narration connects these through a sequence of causes. | Establish an everyday setting, introduce a person with a question, then show the object that creates the confusion. |
| 00:32–01:06 | The same drawn scene persists across several samples; names, framing, and family members change within it. | Reuse a scene and animate the meaningful object or reaction. A new sentence need not mean an entirely new illustration. |
| 01:23–01:53 | A writing exercise and a teasing scene turn biographical information into visible situations. A height annotation supports a corrective aside. | Show the misconception being acted on, then reveal one useful correction with a prop or annotation. Let expressions deliver the humor. |
| 02:04–02:46 | Books, diagrams, a cannon, and a close-up build a recognizable character with interests and ambitions. | Give a recurring shopper or worker recognizable clothing, a goal, and a few reusable poses. Props carry information. |

| 11:00, 19:00–20:00, 27:00–34:00 | Maps, colored territories, flags, and positions explain relationships that character scenes cannot efficiently show. | Switch from the character to a clean diagram, equal-size comparison, timeline, or label close-up when explaining the mechanism. |

The sampled artwork uses bold irregular outlines, simple faces, recognizable clothing, restrained posing, and broad fields of color. Foreground crops and wide settings vary scale. Text mainly identifies people, places, events, and occasional jokes. The narration supplies most of the explanation.

The sequential opening shows sustained illustrations, reframing, detail changes, dissolves, and fades. This is useful evidence that illustrated storytelling does not require continuous full-character animation or a new asset every two seconds. Some sampled dark frames are actual fades, confirmed with sequential decoding. We should use transitions purposefully rather than reproduce their frequency.

The captions contain roughly 8,350 words across 51 minutes (about 164 words per minute averaged over the whole runtime, including gaps). This is a rough density measure, not a speech-rate or pause analysis. The script frequently introduces a circumstance, then a complication, then its consequence; occasional conversational asides break up exposition.

## Direction for the next health draft

Build an original illustrated miniature story with one clear correction. Keep the existing cream, forest-green, and coral brand colors. Use a recurring silent character and off-screen narration; expression and pose changes are enough. Retain the approved Qwen/Fish voice, with performance shaped around curiosity, realization, and a reassuring conclusion.

Create a small character sheet before episode art: front and three-quarter views, neutral/curious/skeptical/relieved expressions, and holding/pointing/thinking poses. Reuse that master design. Use Nano Banana 2 through Google Flow CLI for coordinated scenes and complete illustrated infographics, including integrated headings, labels, callouts, and numbers. The user explicitly prefers using its text-generation capability; generated text is an intended production option. Supply the exact approved wording, values, units, layout, and master visual reference in the prompt.

Choose HTML/SVG for elements that need independent motion, changing values, localization, or precise timed reveals. A complete generated infographic can remain a still and receive camera moves, highlights, or overlays. Generate separate assets only when the scene requires them. Inspect all generated wording, numbers, units, diagram relationships, and phone-size legibility against the approved content; correct or regenerate actual defects before rendering. Keep spoken captions separately timed to narration.

Compose each scene as background, character, prop, and optional diagram layers. Animate a hand reaching, a head turning, a package rotating, a number appearing, or the camera reframing. Keep one dominant action per beat. A change should answer the viewer's question or reveal new information. Full AI video remains optional and silent.

Use short caption phrases for mobile accessibility, distinct from labels inside the scene. Leave space around the character's expression and the evidence being revealed. Plan the vertical composition directly; the reference's landscape frames would lose important context if merely center-cropped.

### Example: a new treatment of the label-reading premise

This is a proposed storyboard, not a replacement export or a revised scientific script. Reuse the existing episode's supported claims and fictional-product disclosure, and re-check sources during production.

| Approximate beat | Visual action | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–4 s | A recurring shopper confidently picks a fictional package with an appealing front claim. | A recognizable decision creates the question. |
| 4–9 s | A close-up catches the shopper's uncertainty; the claim stays attached to the package. | Invite curiosity without mocking the viewer. |
| 9–19 s | A hand flips the package. Background detail softens as the relevant nutrition field and ingredient line become legible. | Show where the useful information is. |
| 19–30 s | Two fictional packages sit beside equal-size portions; HTML numbers demonstrate a like-for-like comparison. | Explain the reasoning visually. Avoid implying one nutrient alone determines overall health. |
| 30–40 s | Return to the shopper comparing the packs and making an informed choice. End on the repeatable label-reading action. | Pay off the opening situation with a practical habit. |

These durations are planning targets; final animation follows the measured approved narration. Place natural breaths between setup, correction, and action. Review the result for a conversational delivery rather than copying this creator's voice or forcing an emotion cue onto every sentence.

For longer videos, repeat this structure in chapters: everyday question → mechanism diagram → common exception → practical application. Reuse the cast and setting to connect chapters. Shorts should resolve one question rather than compress all chapters into a dense list.

## Production check

Before rendering, the storyboard must show: a visible opening situation, one main question, a reveal that changes understanding, and a return to a practical choice. Each frame must have an identifiable focal point at phone size. Verify character proportions, clothing, and palette across scenes. Review a brief motion sample for legibility and the complete encoded export for visual/narration alignment.

The downloaded reference, frames, and captions are study material. Produce original artwork, wording, and narration; keep the creator's footage, drawings, music, and identity out of FitSnap exports. Existing science verification and draft-only publication rules remain in force.
