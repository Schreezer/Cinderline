| Young scientists investigated 15 detox products in 2009 and contacted their makers about definition and evidence. Companies used different definitions; most offered no evidence. A hair straightener was included. | [Sense about Science, Debunking detox](https://senseaboutscience.org/activities/debunking-detox/), opening paragraph and dossier findings. | A small investigation of products, not a representative survey of 15 brands or specifically of teas. “Offered no evidence” describes what investigators received, not proof that no evidence could exist. Fictional scientist and packaging illustrate events; they are not documentary photographs. |
| Detox diets lack convincing evidence for removing toxins. | [NCCIH, Detoxes and Cleanses](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/detoxes-and-cleanses-what-you-need-to-know), research section, updated March 2025. | NCCIH reports few, low-quality human studies and a review finding no compelling support for toxin elimination. Lack of convincing evidence is not proof that every possible product has been tested and failed. |
| Liver processes harmful substances. | [Cambridge University Hospitals NHS, Alcohol and the Liver](https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/alcohol-and-the-liver/), “The functions of your normal liver.” | Normal liver function; not immunity to poisoning or a treatment recommendation. |
| Kidneys filter waste; image also says extra water. | [NIDDK, Your Kidneys and How They Work](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/kidneys-how-they-work), introductory paragraphs and kidney functions. | Describes healthy kidneys. |
| Ask what a cleanse is meant to remove. | Editorial takeaway from the documented ambiguity in the dossier. | Consumer evaluation question, not a guarantee that a specific toxin name proves effectiveness. |
